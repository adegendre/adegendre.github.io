The crisis in science and some of its causes

Some thoughts on the replication crisis and the file drawer problem
Doing Science
Published

November 17, 2026

This post describes my understanding of the crisis in science and some of its causes. This post goes with The Double Failure of Bad Science, where I develop some thoughts on some solutions. I’ve written a bit about this topic a while back in an earlier post.

The recent decade has witnessed a crisis in science and public inquiry into the credibility of scientific findings. Scientific failures are embarrassing and a massive source of waste: waste of public money, waste of time, waste of careers and brains.

In 2015, the Open Science Collaboration found that fewer than half of the published psychology results it tried to reproduce held up. The following year, a team of economists led by Colin Camerer tried to replicate experimental economics papers from the American Economic Review and the Quarterly Journal of Economics, two of the most respected journals in the field. Only about 61 percent of articles replicated. To replicate a study is to run it again, with new participants or new data, and to check whether the same result comes out.

Since then, an emerging body of work has identified several culprits. The first culprit is studies that are too small to reliably detect what they claim to find, often leading to false claims (Ioannidis 2005, PLoS Medicine; Button et al. 2013 for neuroscience). With few participants, chance alone can produce a striking result. The smaller the study, the more likely it is that a striking result is a fluke.

The second culprit is protocols described too thinly for anyone else to faithfully repeat the experiment. A protocol is the full description of the way a study was run: the participants, the tasks they were given, and the way their answers were measured. When that description is incomplete, a second team cannot run the same study.

The third culprit is samples that are not representative of anyone, with most behavioral findings resting on “WEIRD” subjects, usually Western university students (Henrich, Heine and Norenzayan 2010). WEIRD stands for Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, and Democratic. A result found among students at a few Western universities may not hold for the rest of the world.

Replication failure is one particular aspect of the scientific crisis. Another aspect is publication bias: because null effects are much harder to publish than striking results, the published scientific literature in many fields is biased towards attention-grabbing findings. A null effect means that the study found no sign that the treatment or the policy it tested made a difference. In those areas of research, studies with large, striking findings are over-represented, while studies that find null effects often don’t get published at all (the so-called “file drawer problem”). The name comes from the filing cabinet where those studies stay, unread. A policymaker reading the literature is reading a selected slice of the evidence.

Another aspect is scientific fraud. Albeit rare, scientific fraud never fails to make headlines and does contribute to the crisis of confidence (Diederik Stapel; the Gino and Ariely data-fabrication allegations). On the Gino and Ariely case, see the blog pieces by Data Colada (here and here) and the fantastic piece in the New Yorker. In a field where a fraudster has been uncovered, the rest of the field spends years cleaning up while public trust takes the hit. The cleanup can be just as long when no fraud is involved. See Susan Dominus’s New York Times Magazine account of what followed Amy Cuddy’s power-pose research (the claim that holding a confident posture for a couple of minutes changes a person’s hormones and behavior) after it failed to replicate.

Conflicts of interest are a related but distinct issue from fraud. A conflict of interest arises when the people who fund or run a study gain from one particular result. The long-running cultural belief that “breakfast is the most important meal of the day” began in large part as a marketing slogan from cereal manufacturers, including Kellogg’s, in the early twentieth century. It is hard to overstate how much industry-funded nutrition research and pharmaceutical-company studies have shaken the trust people have in medical and life sciences.

Importantly, however, genuine heterogeneity can also explain a “failed” replication. There are real differences across populations, or differences in institutions and incentives. A result that does not replicate in a new place may still be correct in the place where it was first found. I come back to this point in the main post.
