The Double Failure of Bad Science

Some thoughts on bad science and on using evidence with discernment
Doing Science
Published

November 17, 2026

I’ve been thinking a lot about the issues in this post over the past decade. Over the years, I ended up explaining to people around me why I think it’s important to participate in large-scale replications, to pre-register analyses, and so on. I am now writing this down as a way to wrestle with my thoughts, because writing is thinking. It helps me think through the deep reasons that drive me to do these activities.

In 2010, two prominent economists, Carmen Reinhart and Kenneth Rogoff, published a paper called “Growth in a Time of Debt.” Looking at decades of data, they reported a striking pattern: countries with public debt above about 90 percent of gross domestic product (GDP) appeared to grow much slower than those below the threshold. Within months, the European Commission, US politicians, and Treasury officials across several countries were citing the 90 percent threshold to justify measures of austerity.

Three years later, a graduate student and two of his professors at the University of Massachusetts Amherst obtained the underlying spreadsheet. They found two problems: an Excel error that excluded several countries from the calculation, and results that turned out to be sensitive to how those countries’ growth experiences were weighted. Once the error was fixed, the dramatic threshold effect largely disappeared.

People usually tell this story as a scientific mistake. I think it is also about a second, subtler failure: policymakers rushing to implement fiscal austerity without discernment, at immense societal and political cost. Even if the original correlation had been correct, transplanting it across very different countries and historical eras as if it were a law of nature ignored the fundamental uncertainty of any research finding. This double failure durably eroded trust in both political institutions and science.

How much of the “evidence” behind “evidence-based” policy is this fragile? And how much do mistakes like the Reinhart and Rogoff study cost us? Both questions point to a shared duty. Researchers must produce the most reliable work they can. But those who consume that work, policymakers above all, have a responsibility to use it with discernment. A researcher cannot be held accountable for policymakers doing policy without investing the time to understand the evidence base.

Science is in crisis

The case of the Reinhart and Rogoff study is not an isolated incident. The recent decade has witnessed a crisis in science and public inquiry into the credibility of scientific findings.

Science has many issues, but we’ve seen it placarded all over the internet over the past decades. In 2015, the Open Science Collaboration found that fewer than half of the published psychology results it tried to reproduce held up. The following year, a team of economists led by Colin Camerer tried to replicate experimental economics papers from two of the most respected journals in the field. Only about 61 percent of articles replicated.

Beyond replication failure, there is also the issue of publication bias. Publication bias is the fact that the papers that get published are often a selection of the knowledge that is produced. For example, studies with large, striking findings are over-represented in the published literature, while studies that find no effect often don’t get published at all (the so-called “file drawer problem”). The issue with publication bias is that policymakers reading the literature only read a selected part of the evidence. I describe the crisis and my understanding of some of its causes in more detail in a separate post.

Why should we care that science is in crisis? Because bad science is waste in at least four ways.

First, misallocated funding. When the published record is incomplete or distorted, public research dollars chase questions that have already been studied (sometimes already answered negatively) because the relevant null results, the studies that found no effect, are sitting unread in someone else’s filing cabinet. Second, false leads. Like misallocated funding, bad science is a waste of other researchers’ time chasing false leads. Take for example the 2006 Lesné and Ashe paper in Nature, which placed a particular protein (Aβ*56) at the heart of Alzheimer’s and was retracted in 2024 after years of related work had been built on it. The cost is years of researcher-time and careers built on sand. This is especially costly to junior talent. Third, opportunity cost. The grant funding a flawed study is the grant that did not fund a sound one. Fourth, eroded public trust. The saddest and perhaps most telling example: Andrew Wakefield’s 1998 paper linking the measles, mumps, and rubella (MMR) vaccine to autism was retracted in 2010, but the damage to vaccine uptake and the subsequent measles resurgences are still with us.

Scientific Reform: Fixing the production of science

Over the past two decades or so, a growing movement amongst scientists has pushed to improve the way we do science towards more transparency and more credibility. The broad agenda of the Open Science movement is to define best practices that allow the scientific community to catch mistakes throughout the scientific process, from openly sharing code and experimental protocols to systematically auditing published studies.

First, large-scale replications after publication. Many problems do not surface until someone tries to redo a paper that is already published. In economics, the Institute for Replication, launched in 2022, coordinates that work across economics journals, checking whether published findings hold up. The idea is to find mistakes, and to deter future data manipulation or fraud. In a study I took part in, published in Nature in 2026, we took 110 papers from top economics and political-science journals and tried to reproduce them: more than 85 percent could be reproduced from the data and code, but coding errors lurked in about a quarter, and the headline result held up under reasonable robustness checks (running the analysis again in other defensible ways) in only about 70 percent. Beyond catching errors, the work has changed incentives: for authors, knowing your paper may be audited makes you check it more carefully before submitting; for replicators, there are increasingly many avenues to publish their replication study.

Second, checking papers before they are published. Top journals are increasingly verifying before any paper goes to print that the results can be reproduced from the data and code. In economics, the American Economic Association now runs every accepted paper through a dedicated “Data Editor” team that re-runs the analysis. In psychology, the top journal Psychological Science has adopted the same model under the leadership of their editor Simine Vazire, my colleague at the University of Melbourne. This type of initiative catches the kind of error in Reinhart and Rogoff’s paper at the source.

Third, emptying the file drawer. Some fields are moving toward pre-registration and pre-analysis plans. In economics, for example, it is now standard for experimental work to release a document before collecting data (a pre-registration) explaining what the research team will measure and what would count as a meaningful finding. This makes it harder for null results to vanish into a drawer, because the original plan is on the record even if the study never makes it to a journal. Several journals also go further by publishing registered reports, articles accepted for publication solely on the basis of the research design, before any results are in. That way, the studies that find “nothing happened” get published too, and the evidence base is progressively less biased.

Fourth, catching honest mistakes. Artificial intelligence has enabled new tools designed to catch errors before papers go to print. Refine Technologies, an AI “peer reviewer” built by academics at Harvard and Northwestern and launched in 2025, scans manuscripts for inconsistencies and calculation errors in minutes. Tellingly, its founders open every pitch with Reinhart and Rogoff, exactly the kind of spreadsheet slip an automated check would have caught. We still do not know how far AI-based tools can push the credibility of scientific work, but many experts are hopeful that they can enhance the scientific process. See the development economist Edward Miguel’s recent panel on the question and a National Bureau of Economic Research session transcribed by Scott Cunningham.

This is by no means complete, there are many other ways and initiatives to improve science and scientific practices. My understanding is that all of these approaches share one thing in common: they all target the production of research to make new studies more reliable.

I think there is a remaining issue that I don’t see much debate about, which is how consumers of research interact with and interpret findings.

Can we fix the consumption of science?

Consumers of science often have issues understanding scientific findings and deriving policy recommendations from them. The reaction to the latest PISA results shows how much policymakers want to learn from research. PISA is the OECD’s international test of 15-year-olds in reading, mathematics and science. The 2025 results, released in September 2026, recorded the lowest OECD-average scores so far in all three subjects. Since 2022, scores fell in reading and in mathematics in more than half of the countries with comparable data.

Governments reacted within days. In Australia, the Minister for Education, Jason Clare, declared that “the reading wars are over. We know what works to teach children to read,” and pointed to the reforms England rolled out almost twenty years ago. In Germany, the federal education minister, Karin Prien, called the results “dramatic” and said that, unlike at the time of the “PISA shock” of 2000, “today we know the answers.” In France, the education ministry called for a gain of 20 points by 2029, the equivalent of one year of learning. A few days before the release, the Conseil d’analyse économique, which advises the French Prime Minister, had published an action plan of “measures whose effectiveness has been demonstrated by research and international practice.” Slovenia announced a comprehensive reform of its education system, along with a new national institute to study the effects of education policies.

These reactions show that policymakers care deeply about adopting best practice in education policy. The point I make in this post is that we don’t really know how to digest the knowledge from the academic literature and derive best-practice education policies, and we don’t yet have the tools to do it. This is a point I recently made in a Parliamentary inquiry in Australia, and presented in a public hearing last week.

Even when a scientific finding is sound, we can waste it by transporting it badly, generalizing beyond its original context. In a famous study, the economist Eva Vivalt looked at the evidence base on development programs around the world and found that the settings where such programs had been studied were so diverse that taking one estimate and applying it elsewhere could be wrong by roughly 100 percent.

The evidence base behind much of what policymakers cite is thinner than it looks. Take peer effects in education, the idea that the classmates a young person studies alongside matter for what they learn. This topic has been at the heart of the debate on school choice and urban segregation. Yet almost everything we know about peer effects comes from a handful of universities that, for administrative reasons, randomly assigned roommates or classmates. A policymaker in Canberra deciding whether to give families more choice would be extrapolating from a tiny, highly selected sample that does not represent the diversity of Australian school-age children.

When that base is thin, the temptation is to take one famous number from one famous study and assume it applies everywhere else. Class size is the textbook example. Tennessee’s Project STAR experiment in the 1980s found that smaller classes produced large gains for elementary-school students. When California rolled out a similar policy statewide in the 1990s, the gains largely vanished, partly because the state could not hire enough qualified teachers fast enough, and partly because school districts implemented it differently. A minister wanting to know what class-size reduction would do in their schools cannot simply borrow Tennessee’s number.

Quebec’s universal childcare rollout in the late 1990s is another cautionary tale. The province moved an entire cohort of young children into heavily subsidized daycare, only to find a decade later that the program had not been able to maintain quality at scale, and that the cohort showed worse behavioral and mental-health outcomes than its peers in other provinces.

So what can we do about that? How can we fix how we consume scientific findings?

Well, we can start with conducting a meta-analysis on a given topic: we pool together every study on the given research question (say, what is the effect of having higher achieving peers in my classroom on my test scores?) and report the average. That is better than relying on one result from a famous study. But it returns an average across very different settings and often different implementations, when what the policymaker actually needs is evidence that speaks to a setting like theirs. In addition, meta-analysis cannot easily distinguish between differences caused by true heterogeneity (genuine differences between settings and populations) and differences caused by research styles. Some of the variation in research findings is irreducible: the way someone decided to code a variable among many, the way someone defined their main sample, whether they added controls in an analysis. In a recent study I took part in, 146 research teams were given the same dataset and asked the same policy question. Even working from identical numbers, they reached strikingly different conclusions. The spread between teams was large, and tightening the rules (telling teams exactly what statistical method to use) did not close it. What this study tells us is that behind every “the effect was X” there is a distribution of plausible estimates, not a single fact. We need to get comfortable with this uncertainty in the truth.

So when we take an estimate from one study and assume it will transport to a new setting, two things can go wrong at once. One, we ignore the variation that comes from researchers’ choices in producing the original estimate, and two, we ignore the variation that comes from genuine differences between settings and populations.

New approaches for translating findings to policy

I think we need to go further and find new approaches for translating findings to policy. I see two ways to do that: 1) producing all the missing estimates from imperfect data, 2) combining settings and making them comparable.

First, to produce the missing estimates, we can recover data that has been produced but shelved because of imperfect experimentation. Many real-world policy experiments get shelved when the experimentation was not perfectly implemented, leaving expensive, unpublished data sitting in a drawer. Experiments that rely on random matching, such as the assignment of students to classrooms, patients to doctors or cases to judges, often fail in a few schools, hospitals or courts, because some institutions find ways to bypass the rule. With Nicolás Salamanca at the University of Melbourne, we have developed a statistical method, the Fishing Algorithm, to rescue these experiments, salvaging usable estimates from data that would otherwise be thrown away. We implemented this method to salvage a fantastic survey in Taiwan, one of a kind. In Taiwan, national law requires schools to assign students to classrooms at random. Schools do not fully comply with the mandate, and 102 of the 333 schools in the data assign students by ability in breach of it. Our tool fishes out those schools, and the remaining schools show that higher-scoring classmates raise student test scores. More usable experiments mean more evidence about more settings, directly addressing the thinness of data and evidence I described above.

Second, we can also combine settings and harmonize data to make them actually comparable. With my coauthors Ulf Zölitz (University of Zurich), Jan Feld (Victoria University of Wellington) and Nicolás Salamanca (University of Melbourne), we show this idea in our paper on same-sex-teacher effects. We take a single policy question, what happens when boys are taught by male teachers and girls by female teachers, and we estimate this effect across many settings using one consistent approach throughout. Once the methodology is held constant, the differences in our findings across settings reflect genuine differences across schools, classrooms or countries, not different research teams making different choices.

Our study on same-sex teacher effects in education illustrates why we cannot assume that effects from one place will transport to another. In this paper we study three million students in 90 countries, and we find two key results. The first result is that same-sex teacher effects on test scores in primary education are tiny, hover around zero and are positive in only half of the countries and negative in the other half. This means that trying to increase same-sex matching of students and teachers in primary school is a coin flip in terms of policymaking. It also means that foreign estimates may not transport well at all. Our findings indicate that massive hiring of male primary school teachers is not a reliable way to boost test scores for boys.

The second result is that same-sex teacher effects are, in contrast, small but universally positive in other dimensions: in primary school, student enjoyment of subjects and self-confidence, and in secondary school, effects on test scores, subject enjoyment, self-confidence and intentions to go into science, technology, engineering and mathematics (STEM). Whether findings transport from one setting to another is a question we should study, because it’s really not obvious.

The bottom line is that in science, the norm is thin, lopsided, noisy evidence. In many areas of research, the answer to this problem is not to produce one more study. It is a disciplined way to pool what we already have, and to think carefully how results might apply in the next population or setting. We can be much less wasteful with the knowledge and data that we already have.

Responsibility is shared between producers of research and consumers of research. On the production side, researchers are raising the bar: data editors at journals, mass replication after publication, AI tools that catch coding mistakes, pre-registration and registered reports. But now we also need the consumer side to start using evidence with discernment, by learning to accept the uncertainty underlying every scientific finding.

The next spreadsheet error is already out there. Whether it sets policy will depend on both sides doing their job: the producer catching it, and the consumer refusing to build a country’s austerity on a single number that nobody bothered to check.
